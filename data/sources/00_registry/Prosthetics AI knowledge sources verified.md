# Audit Confirms Twelve Sources, Flags Twelve

All 24 first-pass sources resolve to real records, but only **12 are cleanly verified**: 10 are PARTIAL, S004 carries a material error, and S012 cannot be identified beyond a repository stub. The most consequential fix is that the VA/DoD lower-limb guideline (S004) has **26 recommendations, not 18** (the 18 matches the 2017 edition) and was posted in January 2025 under a December 2024 cover date. Other fixes: S003 is peer-reviewed (ICCV 2025), S007 has n=22 and an abstract that reports no significant K-level differences, primary papers now replace the news and CME summaries behind S008 and S010, S015's headline claims are unconfirmed, the registry URLs for S022 and S023 point to the wrong documents, and S024 now has a results preprint. Five new research streams added a tiered list of **294 entries (178 opened and identity-verified at record level, 69 partly opened, 47 preprint, secondary or non-evidence)** plus 21 grouped leads that were never opened and must not be cited, with real anchors: graded guidelines (VA/DoD 2024 lower-limb and 2022 upper-limb, the APTA/ANPT stroke AFO guideline, SOSORT 2016), open datasets (18 above-knee and 30 transtibial amputees, GaitRec's 2,295 subjects, NinaPro's amputee subsets) and primary regulator documents (FDA's January 2026 CDS guidance, MDCG 2019-11 and 2025-6). Certainty is low almost everywhere prescription logic would need it: the sources contained no validated orthosis-selection algorithm, no orthosis-user gait dataset, no prosthetics RAG benchmark, no prosthesis- or orthosis-user validation of OpenCap, Pose2Sim, MMPose or MediaPipe, and no ML study of component selection. Every check ran through summarising fetch tools, and no Crossref, PubMed or Retraction Watch lookup was possible, so "verified" means record-level identity, not a clean bill on retractions or on each quoted number. Nothing here is a clinical recommendation; guideline statements are reported as the guidelines word them, and the corrected registry (Appendix A) and tiered list of new sources (Appendix B) follow the conclusion.

## Twelve sources verify cleanly, but every correction changes how the evidence can be used

The verification method sets the ceiling on every claim below. Page reads went through fetch tools that return a model-written summary rather than raw HTML, so quotations and numbers are "tool-summarised" (one summary printed a participant height as "165±7 m"). Crossref, NCBI E-utilities, ISO.org and Cochrane were blocked by the session egress policy; PMC pages returned reCAPTCHA; PubMed, arXiv v2 and the Europe PMC API returned HTTP 429; ClinicalTrials.gov was refused by robots.txt. No Crossref, PubMed or Retraction Watch check ran for any of the 24 sources, so the observation that publisher pages showed no correction or retraction notice is weak evidence only. Within those limits the tally is 12 VERIFIED (S001, S002, S005, S006, S009, S011, S013, S014, S016, S018, S019, S021, with S013 and S016 needing field corrections), 10 PARTIAL (S003, S007, S008, S010, S015, S017, S020, S022, S023, S024), 1 PROBLEM (S004) and 1 UNVERIFIED (S012). None was fabricated or misidentified, but several were mis-described.

The S004 correction is the largest. The official VA page, the full guideline PDF and the clinician summary PDF all state **26 recommendations** (Version 3.0, cover dated December 2024, PDF file dated 2025-01-10, evidence reviewed through 15 March 2024, GRADE) ([VA/DoD page](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/Rehab/amp/index.asp); [guideline PDF](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/Rehab/amp/LLA-CPG_2024-Guideline_final_20250110.pdf)), and the S005 abstract also says the work group "developed 26 recommendations" ([USUHS record](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/clinicians-guide-to-the-2024-vadod-clinical-practice-guideline-fo/)). The "18" came from the APTA listing text, which also carries an author list (Webster, Pruziner, Randolph) that does not match the 2024 working group ([APTA listing](https://www.apta.org/patient-care/evidence-based-practice-resources/cpgs/clinical-practice-guidelines-for-the-rehabilitation-of-individuals-with-lower-limb-amputation)). A separate notes stream opened the 2017 edition page and found that edition lists exactly 18 recommendations (4 Strong For, 13 Weak For, 1 Neither) ([VA/DoD 2017](https://health.mil/Reference-Center/Presentations/2018/01/09/2017-VA-DoD-Clinical-Practice-Guideline-Rehabilitation-of-Individuals-with-Lower-Limb-Amputation)), which supports reading the APTA figure as a legacy count. That reading remains an inference, because no page states it. By the verifier's own tally of the tool-summarised table, the 2024 guideline has 16 Weak For and 10 Neither-for-nor-against and no Strong recommendations; the wording must be quoted from the PDF itself before any rule is built. The two dates resolve as December 2024 for the document and January 2025 for public release, so the safe citation is "VA/DoD 2024 LLA CPG v3.0, released January 2025", with the corporate author and working-group list rather than the APTA names.

Identity and edition corrections fall into a few families. Peer-review status was understated for S003, which is in the ICCV 2025 proceedings (pp. 8984-8993) even though one arXiv fetch returned a different title and the arXiv date is unverified ([CVF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/html/Yin_ProGait_A_Multi-Purpose_Video_Dataset_and_Benchmark_for_Transfemoral_Prosthesis_ICCV_2025_paper.html)). Secondary summaries were replaced by primary papers for S008 (Dillon et al., Prosthet Orthot Int 2018;42(2):191-197, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0309364617706748)) and S010 (Wada et al., PM&R 2022;14(7):828-836, confirmed on one institutional record only, [Fujita repository](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/the-effect-of-ankle-foot-orthosis-on-ankle-kinematics-in-individu/)). Versions diverge for S016 (the peer-reviewed PLOS version covers 70 studies, the July 2024 preprint 37, so cite PLOS only, [PLOS Digital Health](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pdig.0000877)) and S017 (the arXiv abstract was rewritten across at least v1 to v4, a Research Square copy lists nine rather than ten authors, and no peer-reviewed version was found, [arXiv v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01146v4)). Registry URLs pointed at the wrong document for S022, where the link is ISO/FDIS 10328 Edition 3 (stage 50.20, dated 2026-08-07, so not yet a published standard) rather than ISO 10328:2016 ([ISO 86870](https://www.iso.org/standard/86870.html)), and for S023, where the APTA link is the lower-limb page and the official upper-limb page is at healthquality.va.gov ([VA ULA page](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/Rehab/ULA/)). S024's "no results" is out of date: a medRxiv preprint (Nataletti et al., posted 2025-07-22, not peer reviewed) reports results, though it does not report the protocol's planned ML models and its participant counts conflict between two extractions ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.21.25331936v1.full)). S015 is a real arXiv preprint (Gilon, Miller, Uhlrich), but its abstract as returned reports 4.8 degrees mean absolute rotational error and 3.4 cm pelvis error, and does not state the registry's "comparable to two-camera OpenCap" or "clinically meaningful knee-loading" claims ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24733)).

These corrections propagate into the first-pass rules. Rule R2 leaned on S007 to support recording objective measures alongside K-level, yet S007's abstract reports "no significant differences" between K-level groups in 22 participants, so it is hypothesis-generating at most ([CPOJ copy](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/cpoj/cpoj-37456.pdf)); a Results-section summary showed p=0.04 for hip flexion, which nobody resolved, and the copy read was a third-party mirror. S004's closest statement is recommendation 20 (patient-reported and performance-based measures with acceptable psychometric properties, Weak For), and none of the 26 mentions K-level assignment in the returned text. S008's ">80%" holds for the combined model (85.1% at K2, 84.2% at K3), whereas the AMP-only model reached 79.7% at K2, and the ground truth is itself a single clinician's K-level judgement in a retrospective sample with only 5 K1 and 18 K4 cases ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0309364617706748)). Rule R1's "short-term improvement" comes from S010's conclusion; S009's abstract states no follow-up horizon, its protocol was not registered, and no GRADE rating was seen ([PMC8342539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8342539/)). Rule R3 is on firmer ground: the "not suitable to serve as a guide for the selection of a specific lower limb prosthetic device" wording is on both the FDIS page and the ISO 10328:2016 catalogue page, though both were tool-summarised and the standard is paywalled ([ISO 10328:2016](https://www.iso.org/standard/70205.html)). Finally, S006's model performance should cite n=98 (six of 104 were excluded), RBF-SVM accuracies of 92.59%, 83.67% and 82.99% under 10-fold cross-validation, and no external validation ([MDPI PDF](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/13/22/6763/pdf)); whether class balancing (98 people became 162 samples) happened inside the folds was not confirmed. S012, the CAD/CAM review, is the only source that failed identification: the repository stub shows no author, year, venue or DOI, and the registry's "three 3D printing, one CNC milling" detail was not in the abstract text retrieved ([SDL record](https://drepo.sdl.edu.sa/handle/20.500.14154/51522)).

## Orthotic evidence peaks at stroke AFOs and scoliosis bracing, then thins out

Graded, guideline-level evidence exists for two orthosis areas only. The APTA/ANPT guideline on AFOs and functional electrical stimulation after stroke reviewed 288 studies (272 primary, 16 systematic reviews; search May 2017 to November 2019) and, as extracted, uses "should" statements for gait speed, other mobility and dynamic balance (Strong in chronic and Moderate in acute stroke for gait speed, evidence level I) and a "should NOT" when plantarflexor spasticity is the primary goal (Moderate, level II); the extraction table is flagged CHECK for exact wording, and the DOI was not displayed on either page opened ([JNPT full text](https://journals.lww.com/jnpt/Fulltext/2021/04000/A_Clinical_Practice_Guideline_for_the_Use_of.6.aspx)). For adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, the SOSORT 2016 guideline grades 25 bracing recommendations (2 grade A, 20 grade B, 3 grade C), but its Risser and curve-magnitude thresholds came back garbled and are marked CHECK ([SOSORT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5795289/)). The Cochrane 2015 review (7 studies, 662 adolescents) rates certainty low to very low and finds no quality-of-life difference between bracing and observation, read from the Cochrane summary page because the full abstract returned HTTP 412/429 ([Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/CD006850/MUSKEL_braces-for-idiopathic-scoliosis-in-adolescents)). The "72% vs 48%" BrAIST success figures are known only as quoted inside SOSORT, because the NEJM outcome paper could not be opened and only the trial's design paper was ([BrAIST design](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4536980)).

Below those two anchors, certainty is uniformly low or absent. AFO-after-stroke meta-analyses agree on benefit direction for walking outcomes (S009, and Tyson and Kent 2013 with 13 trials and 334 participants, read only as a PEDro abstract, [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/35050)), while S010 grades its two outcomes low certainty and notes that no included trial reported quality of life, adverse events, falls or activities of daily living. The framing conflict is real: the 2024 LeCursi algorithm paper states there is "insufficient evidence to support the efficacy of AFOs in general" ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/rehabilitation-sciences/articles/10.3389/fresc.2024.1353303/pdf)), whereas the guideline grades several outcomes Strong. The notes attribute this to different outcome sets, appraisal tools and dates rather than contradictory data; that attribution is an inference. Quality-of-life and participation evidence is mixed (4 positive, 1 negative, 3 no relationship across 10 articles) ([Quaresma and McMonagle 2024](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/91635/1/Quaresma-and-McMonagle-2024-the-efficacy-of-lower-limb-orthoses-on-quality-of-life-well-being-and-participation-following-stroke.pdf)), and a dedicated Cochrane review of AFOs for walking after stroke exists only as a 2014 protocol ([CD011249](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011249/information)). For knee-ankle-foot and hip-knee-ankle-foot orthoses, the AAOP evidence note (2009) found "no substantive evidence to support or preclude" use for ambulation ([AAOP](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/aaop/pdfs-and-documents/Evidence_Notes/KAFOs_HKAFOs_Ambulation.pdf)), and a 2025 conference-poster scoping review of 10 observational studies rates KAFO certainty after stroke as very low ([World Physiotherapy](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/current-evidence-knee-ankle-foot-orthosis-therapy-patients-stroke-scoping)). Multiple sclerosis has only a 14-paper conference-abstract review comparing AFO with FES ([Davidson and Bowers 2013](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/49064)).

Paediatric and upper-limb evidence is framed differently by different sources. A 2011 consensus states that AFOs controlling the foot and ankle in stance and swing improve gait efficiency in ambulant GMFCS I-III children and that little high-quality evidence supports hip, spine or upper-limb orthoses ([Morris 2011](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/40443/)); a 2024 review of 11 RCTs (442 participants) reports general improvement with AFOs but high risk of bias in ten of the 11 and seven different orthosis types ([Miccinilli 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10887911/)); the 2019 traffic-light review rates AFOs yellow and serial casting green, with rationale wording flagged CHECK ([Novak 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11910-020-1022-z)); and NICE NG62 gives no orthosis recommendations ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng62/chapter/Recommendations)). Upper-limb orthoses after stroke showed no effect on function, range of movement or pain in four trials (126 participants) ([Tyson and Kent 2011](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1451446/the-effect-of-upper-limb-orthotics-after-stroke-a-systematic-review)), and a 2024 Cochrane review of contracture management found very low-certainty evidence with pain and skin breakdown reported as adverse effects ([Cochrane 2024](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010779.pub2/pdf/CDSR/CD010779/CD010779_abstract.pdf)). One 2024 systematic review of 20 papers covers thoracolumbar fractures ([Karimi 2024](https://mjiri.iums.ac.ir/article-1-8927-en.html)); no limitations section or GRADE rating was retrieved, so treat it as a single unappraised review.

For a decision-support build, three findings matter most. No validated deficit-to-orthosis selection algorithm exists in the sources opened; the closest are the guideline's outcome- and phase-specific action statements, SOSORT's stratification by skeletal maturity, curve size and wear hours, and the LeCursi five-step adjustment algorithm, which was demonstrated on two hypothetical cases and not trial-validated ([LeCursi 2024](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-proposed-evidence-guided-algorithm-for-the-adjustment-and-optim/)). Outcome-measure guidance is best for custom AFOs: a scoping review of 82 instruments across 79 articles found five with adequate psychometrics and feasibility (10-metre walk, 6-minute walk, Berg Balance Scale, Timed Up and Go, Rivermead Mobility Index) and found no suitable experience-of-care instrument ([Fatone 2020](https://www.sralab.org/sites/default/files/downloads/2021-06/Fatone%202020%20article.pdf)). And AI work on orthoses is early: a 17-patient automated night-brace design study ([Guy 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10853218)), a 490-patient deep-learning progression model that works from radiographs and not from orthosis choice (accuracy 76.6%, sensitivity 75.2%, specificity 80.2%, [Wang 2021](https://doaj.org/article/05560692eab0409ca7a6a2c547cac91d)), a 2026 review of 17 papers concluding there is no validated end-to-end clinical generative workflow ([Monim 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/94B3B8AD01977D1A63A2B627360D37C6/core-reader)), and a funded ML-AFO project with no posted results ([Stevens project](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/projects/machine-learning-methods-to-individualize-powered-orthotic-interv-3/)). The only open clinical dataset found is a 785-adolescent braced-scoliosis file on Zenodo whose data dictionary was not checked ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/5896952)). Because orthosis harms are barely reported (no contraindication list from ISPO, AAOP or a guideline was found, and no dedicated orthosis-complication review was opened), the absence of a listed complication cannot be read as evidence of safety. ISPO and AAOP resources located are pre-2015 and low-certainty by their own statements ([ISPO Hanoi 2007](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/48896/1/Orthotics_Consensus_conference_report.pdf); [WHO 2017](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789241512480)). Two trade or vendor items (the HKU "4D Brace" page and an MSK Doctors opinion piece) carry no data and are not evidence, and one magazine summary mislabels Tyson and Kent as a Cochrane review ([LER](https://lermagazine.com/?p=884)).

## Lower-limb component evidence is weak-for or insufficient, and outcome-measure benchmarks come from narrow samples

The guideline layer has moved since the first-pass search, which matters for any component-selection rule. The 2017 VA/DoD edition, as summarised, offered microprocessor knees over non-microprocessor knees (Rec 15, Weak For per the notes), listed outcome measures including CHAMP, AMP, the 10-metre and 6-minute walk tests (Rec 16), and stated insufficient evidence for socket design, foot categories and suspensions ([VA/DoD 2017](https://health.mil/Reference-Center/Presentations/2018/01/09/2017-VA-DoD-Clinical-Practice-Guideline-Rehabilitation-of-Individuals-with-Lower-Limb-Amputation)). In the 2024 edition, as returned by the verifier, Rec 17 suggests microprocessor knees for prosthetic ambulators (Weak For), Rec 18 finds insufficient evidence to prescribe any specific ESAR or microprocessor foot over another, Rec 19 suggests ESAR or microprocessor feet over SACH (Weak For), Recs 15 and 16 find insufficient evidence for any specific transfemoral socket design or for ischial-containment versus sub-ischial designs, and Rec 20 covers outcome measures ([guideline PDF](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/Rehab/amp/LLA-CPG_2024-Guideline_final_20250110.pdf)). The 2017 blanket statement of insufficient evidence for foot categories has thus been replaced by a more differentiated 2024 text (an inference from comparing two tool-summarised extractions). Other guideline-level sources are the Dutch 2020 guideline (12 key questions, 59 studies, searches to March 2019; individual recommendations were not visible on the page opened, [Fard et al.](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/amputation-and-prosthetics-of-the-lower-extremity-the-2020-dutch-/)), a Colombian guideline with 43 recommendations of which 93% rest on low or very low evidence ([Posada-Borrero 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/rehabilitation-sciences/articles/10.3389/fresc.2022.873436/pdf)), and the CMS 2017 workgroup document that defines K0-K4 for coverage ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/Medicare/Coverage/Determinationprocess/Downloads/Llp_Consensus_Document.pdf)).

Microprocessor knees carry the only lower-limb component recommendation, and the evidence around it conflicts by context rather than resolving. An AAOP critically appraised topic on K2 transfemoral users appraised six studies and concluded current evidence supports effective intervention, while listing small samples and short-term focus as limitations ([AAOP CAT](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/aaop/CATs/cat_greely.pdf)); the CMS workgroup judged the K2 microprocessor-knee literature "significantly flawed" and could not define the population; and the Hahn 2022 meta-analysis that the appraisal includes was opened only as an industry-affiliated lecture abstract, with the journal paper unopened ([OTWorld 2022](https://congress.ot-world.com/ot_congress/otworld_2022/p5___passteile/hahn_the_effect_of_microprocessor_contro/index_eng.html)). Energy-cost findings split the same way: Highsmith 2012 reported Grade D evidence of improved efficiency while Sawers and Hafner 2013 reported equivalent ambulatory oxygen cost, both known only through an AAOP appraisal ([AAOP CAT](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/aaop/CATs/CAT_Highsmith_Leg_Bioenerget.pdf)). For feet, the 2004 Cochrane review is known through a PEDro record (23 trials, 217 participants, flagged CHECK) and reports insufficient evidence for any single mechanism ([PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/15869)), and an ESR-versus-SACH meta-analysis found a statistically significant but small difference (ESR energy cost 97.3% of SACH), with authors unconfirmed ([Epistemonikos](https://www.epistemonikos.org/en/documents/5bec08b556e9b11fe0e5fd358e01bdf453f1616e)). Socket and suspension reviews (17 papers, 19 articles, four studies on elevated vacuum) call for larger trials ([Ahuaili 2019](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/70565); [AAOP CAT](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/aaop/CATs/CAT_lee.pdf)).

Outcome-measure numbers carry population labels that must travel with them. The AAOP toolkit (2020) lists EQ-5D-5L, Socket Comfort Score and PLUS-M as patient-reported measures and 10mWT, TUG and AMPPRO/AMPnoPRO as performance measures ([AAOP toolkit](https://www.oandp.org/-lower-limb-prosthetics-outcome-measures-toolkit-summary)), and the ISPO COMPASS Delphi (2023) proposes a six-measure core toolkit whose measure names were not visible on the page opened ([Tan et al.](https://www.omicsdi.org/dataset/biostudies-literature/S-EPMC9945573)). AMP means by K-level (for example AMPPRO K2 34.7, K3 40.5) come from a Shirley Ryan database page that cites Gailey 2002, which was not opened ([SRALab](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/amputee-mobility-predictor)); a 6MWT rule of 130 m for K2 and 385 m for K3 versus K1 (sensitivity and specificity near 90%) comes from a 120-person transtibial conference abstract ([EFRR 2019](https://www.egms.de/static/en/meetings/efrr2019/19efrr038.shtml)); a CHAMP score of 33 or more marks performance comparable to servicemembers without limb loss, in male military participants ([Gailey 2013](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Development-and-reliability-testing-of-the/991031560275402976)); and PLUS-M's minimal clinically important difference is "Not established" ([KT summary](https://www.sunnaas.no/4ab5a2/siteassets/seksjon/rkr/documents/plus-m-kt-summary-final.pdf)). The 2MWT predicts the 6MWT (R2 = 0.91, n=86) ([Reid 2015](https://medicaljournals.se/jrm/content/html/10.2340/16501977-1916)), and PLUS-M-TUG associations differ by sample (r = 0.40 in a Korean all-male traumatic cohort of 32, rs = -0.56 in a US conference sample of 118) ([Kim 2025](https://synapse.koreamed.org/articles/1516090588); [Clemens 2018](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/the-component-timed-up-and-go-test-the-utility-and-psychometric-p/)). Primary papers for AMP, the L Test, PLUS-M development, Kannenberg 2014 and Sawers and Hafner 2013 were never opened.

Complication data are heterogeneous and cannot populate a contraindication table. Phantom limb pain prevalence is pooled at 64% across 39 datasets but with I2 of 95.95% and mostly moderate-to-high risk of bias ([Limakatso 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0240431)); a 2006 systematic review found only one study meeting quality criteria for skin problems (16% prevalence in 45 amputees aged 65 or older) while a 2011 Dutch survey had 507 of 805 respondents reporting problems, at a response rate near 39.5% ([Meulenbelt 2006](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/6695973/Meulenbelt_2006_Disabil_Rehabil.pdf); [Meulenbelt 2011](https://www.medicaljournals.se/acta/content/abstract/10.2340/00015555-1023)); and fall figures (20.3% fell in a 315-person service review, 2.81 falls per 1,000 patient days for fluid-controlled knees in inpatients) use incompatible designs ([McGrath 2022](https://doaj.org/article/429f02bc33794c50b4fa60c7836ef9e2); [Palumbo 2022](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/894496)). The AI/ML studies located in prosthetics are feasibility work: smartphone-sensor fall-risk classifiers with only 48.1% sensitivity for actual fallers ([Juneau 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/5/1749)), transfemoral alignment models trained on 16 amputees and validated on 2 ([Cardenas Torres 2023](https://redcol.minciencias.gov.co/Record/UDEA2_91c9c5059a40d620f85bbf094371c3fa/Description)), an automated-alignment systematic review ([Khamis 2024](https://knova.um.edu.my/research_publications_2021_2025/5808)), and socket-rectification models. A classification-tree study of 2,770 users (87.4% training and 81.6% testing accuracy) is known only through an O&P Edge news summary ([O&P Edge](https://opedge.com/classification-tree-predicts-prosthesis-users-functional-potential/)). No peer-reviewed ML study of component prescription was found, so a prescription module would have no ML-validated benchmark.

## Open gait data cover 58 amputee subjects, and markerless joint-angle error sits near 5-6 degrees

Amputee data are the scarcest layer. The verified open sets are S001 (18 above-knee users), S002 (30 transtibial users), a 7-person above-knee Össur Power Knee set that was seen only as a listing on a lab page ([LocoLab](https://locolab.robotics.umich.edu/data.html)) and a 3-person powered knee-ankle case series on IEEE DataPort that requires a free login ([IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/open-access/effects-powered-knee-ankle-prosthesis-amputee-hip-compensations-case-series)), roughly 58 subjects in total and two amputation levels. Nothing verified covers clinical orthosis users: the orthosis-related datasets are a healthy-adult knee-immobilisation study with metabolic data (21 participants, [Bacek 2024](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/a-biomechanics-and-energetics-dataset-of-neurotypical-adults-walk/)) and exoskeleton datasets. Larger references exist but do not confirm amputee or orthosis subgroups: GaitRec holds 2,295 subjects and 75,732 bilateral trials under CC BY 4.0 ([GaitRec](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0481-z)), and the AddBiomechanics core dataset holds 273 participants under CC BY 4.0, described in an arXiv preprint whose amputee content is unstated ([AddBiomechanics](https://addbiomechanics.org/download_data.html)). Stroke coverage includes 138 able-bodied adults plus 50 stroke survivors (licence unconfirmed, abstract-level read, [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/7672c4dbb48348c2a46ea0883d43dbc9)) and four post-stroke participants on Zenodo with no licence shown ([Simonetti](https://zenodo.org/records/8360018)). Licences need per-record checks: several records (Camargo, Simonetti, Molinaro, Elery) did not show permissive terms.

S001 and S002 carry details that the registry should not repeat uncritically. The Nature page reports 67 reflective markers on 15 segments for S001, whereas the gait stream's read said 61, and the CC BY 4.0 statement may be the article licence, not the data licence; the figshare DOI in the registry is only a metadata record ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0494-7)). For S002 the Figshare API reports CC BY 4.0 while the Nature page summary shows CC BY-NC-ND 4.0; the verifier trusted the API as the more direct record for the data files ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-024-03677-3); [Figshare](https://api.figshare.com/v2/articles/25698006)). S003 is gated on Hugging Face under CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 with its baseline code marked "TBD" in the repository README at fetch time ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ericyxy98/ProGait); [GitHub](https://github.com/pittisl/ProGait)).

The tools are open and documented, but validation stops at healthy participants. OpenSim (Apache-2.0), OpenCap, Pose2Sim (BSD-3-Clause; README claims 2-6 degrees, a project claim), OpenSense, MMPose and MediaPipe all have public code or documentation ([OpenSim](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pcbi00/1006223.html); [Pose2Sim](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.04362); [MMPose](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmpose); [MediaPipe](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/pose_landmarker)). OpenCap's original validation used ten healthy adults, and the text returned did not address prostheses or pathological gait ([PLOS Comput Biol](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)); an independent meta-analysis of 12 studies (n=184, mostly healthy 18-35 year olds) pooled an RMSE of 5.877 degrees ([Cabuk 2026](https://www.termedia.pl/Can-OpenCap-deliver-valid-and-reliable-kinematic-data-for-motion-analysis-A-systematic-review-and-three-level-meta-analysis,78,56930,1,1.html)), and a second stream quoted the PLOS paper's own figure as 4.5 degrees mean absolute joint-angle error, a difference the notes do not reconcile ([PLOS Comput Biol](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). The OpenCap code repository is named stanfordnmbl/opencap-core in two notes and opencap-org/opencap-core in a third; the organisation rename or fork status was not checked ([GitHub](https://github.com/opencap-org/opencap-core)). The only prosthesis-specific pose-estimation evidence is a two-subject proof of concept in which OpenPose showed about twice the coordinate error on prosthetic versus intact limbs and a diffusion-based method cut error by 37% (transtibial) and 76% (transfemoral); it is an arXiv preprint of unconfirmed peer-review status, not suited to real-time use ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07854v1)). The only amputee IMU validation is Rattanakoch 2023 (30 transtibial users, intra-subject SD under 1 degree for both systems), read at abstract level ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/51a611f9689e401285f3e820bd826e75)).

The markerless reviews converge on a usable pattern with a systematic caveat. Spatiotemporal parameters are valid, sagittal joint angles are moderate (agreement clusters around 5-6 degrees), ankle and frontal/transverse angles are poor, and pathological populations are rarely tested ([Scataglini 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/11/3686); [Wade 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/12995); [Delaplace preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18667)). For IMUs, Kobsar 2020 (82 articles) rated step and stride time excellent whereas Prisco 2025 (32 studies) reported greater inconsistency for spatiotemporal parameters; the notes present both and no pooled prosthesis- or orthosis-specific result exists ([Kobsar 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12984-020-00685-3); [Prisco 2025](https://doaj.org/article/5f3f441ea02e4b969a836d18ce8220f8)). ML evidence for pathological gait is thin: a CNN-LSTM classifier whose amputee result rests on one person (F1 0.92 with one IMU, 0.95 with two, [Marcos Mazon 2022](https://doaj.org/article/00a5fff9191746c39ac317474c464326)), a kNN insole gait-phase detector on five participants (81.43% accuracy) whose amputee status is unclear ([Rattanasak 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/11/4242)), and a stroke AFO-need predictor (474 patients, AUC 0.887) that uses clinical variables, not gait signals ([Kim 2021](https://oak.ulsan.ac.kr/handle/2021.oak/7992)). A gait-phase review of 84 articles and a machine-learning-in-biomechanics chapter both warn about lab-specific variation and improper cross-validation, which is a direct leakage risk when splitting by trial instead of by subject ([Vu 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/14/3972); [Dindorf 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03717v1)). Reference knowledge is sparse: a 2009 transtibial gait review, a Portuguese energy-expenditure review and the Gait Profile Score (validated on 407 children), with no verified reference values for prosthetic gait by K-level and no validated energy-expenditure prediction model for amputees ([Soares 2009](https://periodicosapm.emnuvens.com.br/spmj/article/view/1901); [Baker 2009](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1452596/the-gait-profile-score-and-movement-analysis-profile)).

## Medical-AI safety evidence is deep, prosthetics-specific evidence is nearly absent, and regulation turns on inputs

The evaluation literature offers components but no complete stack. MIRAGE and the MedRAG toolkit (7,663 questions, 41 corpus-retriever-LLM combinations, gains up to 18% over chain-of-thought) measure answer accuracy, not citation faithfulness ([Xiong 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.372/)). SourceCheckup found that between 50% and 90% of responses from seven LLMs were not fully supported by the sources they cited, with its automated verifier agreeing with expert consensus 88.7% of the time ([Wu 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58551-6)). A large expert-annotation preprint reports that only 22% of the top-16 retrieved passages were relevant in standard RAG, and that filtering and query reformulation improved performance by up to 12% ([Kim et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06738)). The peer-reviewed S016 review adds that most of its 70 studies lacked standardised evaluation and did not address ethics ([PLOS Digital Health](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pdig.0000877)). RAGAS, HealthBench and Med-HALT are useful metric sources, but the notes could not confirm peer review for them ([RAGAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217); [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775); [Med-HALT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15343)). No prosthetics, orthotics or rehabilitation RAG benchmark was found, and none of the verified evaluation methods has been validated on such content.

Documented failure modes map onto the project's risks, though none was measured in prosthetics. Fabricated citations occurred in 55% of 222 GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of 414 GPT-4 citations in a multidisciplinary test ([Walters and Wilder 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)); embedding fabricated clinical details in prompts produced hallucination rates of 50-82% across six LLMs, and a prompt-level mitigation lowered the mean from 66% to 44% ([Omar 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01021-3)); in a test of five frontier LLMs with illogical drug-equivalence requests, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o and GPT-4 complied 100% of the time at baseline and Llama3-8B 94% ([Chen 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-02008-z)); and clinicians' accuracy fell when AI advice was wrong (mammography readers 19.8-45.5% versus roughly 80% when correct, [Dratsch 2023](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiol.222176)). Explanations did not reliably rescue accuracy in a randomised vignette study (systematically biased predictions -11.3 percentage points, with explanations +2.3 and not significant), but that abstract came through OpenAlex because the JAMA page was blocked ([OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1001/jama.2023.22295)). No peer-reviewed study of LLMs recommending superseded guidance was found, only adjacent evidence.

Open-source building blocks exist but maintenance is unverified. Verified repositories include MedRAG (licence type not shown), KG_RAG (Apache-2.0), Medical-Graph-RAG (MIT), Microsoft GraphRAG (MIT, general-purpose), PrimeKG (MIT, README says superseded by OptimusKG, molecular rather than musculoskeletal), RAGAS (Apache-2.0), scispaCy, medspaCy, the CQL toolchain, the HL7 CPG-on-FHIR IG v2.0.0 and CDS Hooks ([MedRAG](https://github.com/Teddy-XiongGZ/MedRAG); [KG_RAG](https://github.com/BaranziniLab/KG_RAG); [PrimeKG](https://github.com/mims-harvard/PrimeKG); [HL7 CPG IG](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/cpg/); [CDS Hooks](https://cds-hooks.org/)). Commit dates could not be fetched, so only stars, forks, releases and archived status support maintenance claims. Meditron's code is Apache-2.0 but its weights carry the Llama 2 Community License, and its developers recommend against clinical deployment without alignment testing ([Meditron](https://github.com/epfLLM/meditron)). No verified project combines rule-based eligibility logic with RAG for prosthetics, and no LLM-based guideline-to-knowledge-graph study was found.

Existing prosthetics and orthotics decision-support systems are few and early. A rule-based Thai prescription CDSS was tested in a 37-participant simulation crossover with two vignettes each; component-level accuracy moved from a median 40% to 60% and decision time from 144 to 120 seconds, neither statistically significant, with a System Usability Scale mean of 63.5 ("marginal usability"), and peer-review status was not stated ([JTMI](https://he03.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/jtmi/article/view/5814)). ProsthetiX-AI is known only from a trade-press article naming no institutions or authors, with the primary paper unlocated ([OPEdge](https://opedge.com/researchers-develop-ai-prosthesis-recommendation-system/)). The socket-design studies at the University of Washington and Radboudumc are known through news pages, though Cutti 2024 was later opened at MDPI ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-1592/6/5/83)). The only verifiably regulated AI product found in the field is Coapt's pattern-recognition controller (FDA 510(k) K191083, decision 24 May 2019), which is a control function, not prescription support ([FDA](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm?ID=K191083)). No orthosis-prescription AI or CDSS and no commercial-vendor documentation was verified.

Regulatory categories depend on inputs and intended purpose (this is not legal advice). Under FDA's January 2026 CDS guidance, software escapes the device definition only if it meets four statutory criteria: no image or signal processing, displays or analyses medical information, supports a health-care professional's recommendation, and lets the professional independently review the basis of the recommendation; automation bias is discussed under criterion 4 in the PDF read, single-recommendation cases fall under enforcement discretion, and the text does not mention generative AI ([FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)). The version history is unsettled: the PDF says issued 29 January 2026 and supersedes a 6 January 2026 version, and two law-firm summaries disagree on whether automation bias is discussed at all, with the notes preferring the primary document ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/5-key-takeaways-from-fdas-revised-clinical-decision-support-cds-software-guidance); [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-20-automation-bias-and-clinical-practice-fda-makes-incremental-updates-to-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)). The hardware classes (21 CFR 890.3420 and 890.3475, Class I and exempt from premarket notification) do not classify a software function, and whether a 3D limb scan counts as a "medical image" is unresolved. In the EU, MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 treats patient-specific decision support as medical device software, and Rule 11 makes decision-support software class IIa by default and higher where harm could be serious ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)); MDCG 2025-6 explains that a device AI system is high-risk under AI Act Article 6(1) only if it is subject to third-party conformity assessment ([MDCG 2025-6](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b78a17d7-e3cd-4943-851d-e02a2f22bbb4_en?filename=mdcg_2025-6_en.pdf)). The AI Act as enacted set 2 August 2026 for general application and 2 August 2027 for Annex I product rules ([Art. 113](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-113)); a 2026 omnibus reportedly moved Annex I to 2 August 2028 and Annex III to 2 December 2027, but that rests on three consultancy or law-firm pages and the EUR-Lex text of Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 was not opened ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement)). The claim that class IIa or higher normally involves a notified body is general MDR knowledge that was not verified in the documents read. Reporting and assurance frameworks are well sourced: TRIPOD-LLM, TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST+AI, CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, DECIDE-AI, FUTURE-AI, NIST AI RMF and CHAI/Joint Commission guidance, several of them through OpenAlex metadata because publisher pages were blocked. No UK MHRA, Health Canada or Syrian regulator sources were sought.

## Digital fabrication and myoelectric evidence rests on small, low-quality studies

For digital sockets and orthoses, no review offers robust superiority evidence and cost data are largely absent. The 2026 scoping review of 20 studies (497 participants; 18 low quality, 2 moderate, none high) found that hybrid and digital methods "may reduce production time and the number of socket attempts" with "modest gains in socket fit and patient comfort", which the authors call "highly tentative", and there were no hybrid-versus-digital comparisons ([van Gastel 2026](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_4a4dbdcb-1e57-499d-91d0-f610b8634a8e)). S013 (11 studies, 177 participants, one rated Good, none on above-knee devices) concludes there is insufficient high-quality evidence for broad conclusions ([PMC12565071](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565071)); a CADTH rapid review found zero studies on 3D-printed liners ([CADTH](https://www.cadth.ca/sites/default/files/pdf/htis/2019/RA1032%203D%20Printed%20Prosthetics%20Final.pdf)); and a single-author review reports 2.5 hours versus 30 minutes for socket fabrication and 32% lower ultimate failure strength for printed composite sockets under ISO 10328 Condition II, which is a bench and workflow report and not a pooled effect size ([Gutierrez 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11168592/)). For 3D-printed AFOs, two reviews (11 studies with n=1 to 8 and GRADE very low; 10 studies) report comparable gait parameters and higher satisfaction with weak evidence ([Wojciechowski 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13047-019-0321-6); [Pollen 2025](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-3d-printed-ankle-foot-orthoses-on-gait-a-systematic-re/)). S011 and S012 belong in this cluster, with S011's failure-strength conclusion limited to "could not be established" and a "comparable up to P8" nuance, and S012 unusable ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0275161)).

AI-driven socket design uses 15 to 118 cases per study, mostly transtibial, and open assets concentrate in shape and finite-element models. Cutti 2024 trained PCA-based rectification templates on 14 people and validated on one, reporting angle errors under 4 degrees and a 0.65% volumetric difference ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-1592/6/5/83)); Jordaan et al. (118 patients; random forest median surface-to-surface distance 1.24 mm) is an arXiv preprint whose page lists a 2026 journal reference that was not opened ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16818)); and the one comparative comfort trial found no significant difference between evidence-generated and clinician-led CAD/CAM sockets (median 8.6 versus 8.8, P=.43, 17 participants) ([Mbithi 2025](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/493705/4/rehab-2025-1-e69962.pdf)), which shows non-inferiority, not superiority. Southampton's OpenLimbTT (33 scans; data CC-BY-SA-4.0, code MIT) and FEA-surrogate datasets are the genuinely reusable open resources ([GitHub](https://github.com/abel-research/openlimb); [Sunderland 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/data-centric-engineering/article/openlimbtt-a-transtibial-residual-limb-shape-model-for-prosthetics-simulation-and-design-creating-a-statistical-anatomic-model-using-sparse-data/3F3A243D8DBAB01D21281BFCEDF9608F)), though one Southampton dataset readme shows conflicting DOI strings (D0484 in the text, D0848 in the file name). No paired scan-to-socket clinical data, open model code for Cutti, Jordaan or van der Stelt, or AI design work for transfemoral, upper-limb or AFO shapes with open code was found.

Upper-limb work adds the tightest constraints. Amputee EMG data are scarce: NinaPro DB3 holds 11 transradial amputees, DB7 and DB8 two each, and DB10 (MeganePro) 15, and most other open EMG sets (Hyser, GRABMyo, putEMG, EMG-EPN-612, UCI) are able-bodied only ([NinaPro](https://ninapro.hevs.ch/); [Atzori 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4421935); [Cognolato 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0380-3)). The 2014 data descriptor reports amputee classification accuracy of 46.27% versus about 75% for intact subjects, and licence terms for DB1-DB10 were not confirmed: the homepage states none, the article is CC BY 4.0, and putEMG is non-commercial (CC BY-NC 4.0). The control-method reviews agree that offline accuracy above 90-95% does not transfer to amputee use ([Parajuli 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6832440); [Kyranou 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2018.00058/full)). Randomised evidence is a single 8-subject transhumeral TMR crossover trial in which 7 of 8 preferred pattern recognition ([Hargrove 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-14386-w)) plus a two-person transradial case study, and the NHS England policy notes very low certainty with samples of 5 to 75 per group ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2009-cpag-summary-report-multi-grip-prosthetic-hand.pdf)).

The upper-limb guideline evidence carries an unresolved count conflict. The official VA page and the ASHT-hosted guideline copy state 14 recommendations in two algorithms, with the copy's ratings read as 4 Weak For and 10 Neither in one note and as "only 2 Weak For" in another ([ASHT PDF](https://asht.org/sites/asht/files/images/International/March%202024/VADoD%20CPG%20Management%20of%20Upper%20Limb%20Amputation.pdf)); the 2023 synopsis abstract says 18 recommendations with 4 having sufficient evidence ([Highsmith 2023](https://www.centreforevidence.org/article/20230301-management-of-upper-limb-amputation-rehabilitation-synopsis-of-the-2022-us-department-of-veterans-affairs-and-us-department-of-defense-clinical-practice-guideline-for-acquired)); and a Hanger press release says the guideline "reinforced 14 prior recommendations and added 3 new ones" ([Hanger](https://corporate.hanger.com/news/2022/hanger-institute-and-department-of-veteran-affairs-release-complementary-guidelines-for-upper-limb-amputations/)). The official VA PDF was never opened, so this is unresolved. Within the copy read, Rec 7 (body-powered or externally powered prosthesis, Weak For) and Rec 8 (insufficient evidence for any specific control strategy, socket, suspension or component) are the component statements. Outcome-measure sources are stronger than component sources: 25 validated task-based assessments (24 of 25 without integrated patient feedback) ([Siegel 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2023.1292632/full)) and the AAOP note naming the Jebsen-Taylor, Box and Block, ACMC, UEFS and TAPES as strongest ([AAOP](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/aaop/pdfs-and-documents/Evidence_Notes/upper-limb_prosthetic_outcom.pdf)), while OPUS has no primary validation source opened. Abandonment statistics are not comparable: 6% discontinued in a one-year veteran cohort of 585 ([Resnik 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0309364620957920)) versus 39-53% rejection figures quoted secondhand from older surveys ([Park 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10295467)). Structural-testing standards are ISO 10328 (2016 edition current, Edition 3 at FDIS), ISO 22675:2024 for ankle-foot devices and ISO 22523:2006 (flagged for revision) ([ISO 22675](https://www.iso.org/standard/79620.html); [ISO 22523](https://committee.iso.org/standard/37546.html)); no ISO or ASTM standard specific to printed sockets or to upper-limb structural testing was found.

## Unresolved conflicts and blocked checks define what must be re-verified before build

Four kinds of risk remain. First, method risk: every number is tool-summarised, the fetch tools sometimes returned garbled or contradictory extractions (a "Demo Journal of Physical Therapy" placeholder DOI on one mirror, a journal name that flips between "Applied Informatics and Computing" and "Artificial Intelligence and Computing" for S019, garbled funder text for S011), and retraction status was checked nowhere beyond publisher pages. Second, access risk: primary papers that were never opened include Gailey 2002 (AMP), Deathe and Miller 2005 (L Test), the PLUS-M development papers, Kannenberg 2014, Sawers and Hafner 2013, Hahn 2022, the NEJM BrAIST outcome paper, the Kuiken 2009 JAMA page, the official VA upper-limb PDF, EUR-Lex texts, the ClinicalTrials.gov record for NCT03930199, and the PubMed or PMC pages for S001, S006, S007 and others. Third, reuse risk: NinaPro states no licence; ProGait is CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 and gated; putEMG is non-commercial; OpenCap Monocular is PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0 with SMPL/SMPL-X body-model restrictions; Meditron weights sit under the Llama 2 Community License; several gait datasets show no licence; and S001's data licence and S002's licence conflict remain unconfirmed. Fourth, label risk: K-level ground truth in S006, S008 and S024 is a clinician judgement, S006's and S008's populations are single-site, and US veteran or military samples underlie CHAMP, AMP norms and the upper-limb abandonment data.

Coverage gaps are as important as conflicts. No amputee or orthosis validation exists for OpenCap, Pose2Sim, MMPose or MediaPipe, no open orthosis-user gait dataset, no open amputee plantar-pressure, balance or metabolic dataset, no orthosis-prescription framework validated against outcomes, no formal contraindication list from ISPO, AAOP or a guideline, no peer-reviewed ML for component prescription, no prosthetics RAG benchmark, no validated guideline-to-knowledge-graph study, no verified orthosis AI/CDSS, and no ASTM orthosis standard was searched. Some absences reflect an exhausted 200-call search budget in two streams, so absence is not proof.

## Conclusion

The audit changes the project's central risk. It is less that sources are fake (none was) and more that first-pass records were stale, mis-versioned or built on secondary summaries: an 18-versus-26 recommendation count, a URL pointing to an unpublished draft standard, a preprint that had become a conference paper, and a results paper that had appeared after the protocol was logged. A knowledge base that stores version, retrieval date, tool-summarised flag and verification tier beside every knowledge item would have caught all of these, and the tiers in Appendix B are designed to be that field. The guidelines themselves supply the right storage pattern, since each statement pairs a strength with evidence certainty and most of them are Weak For or insufficient, which means most prosthetics and orthotics rules the lab could encode today are low-certainty statements that must carry that label.

The more important implication concerns where the lab's own data will matter. Open validation of markerless gait tools stops at healthy adults, open amputee gait data total about 58 people, orthosis-user data are absent, and no benchmark measures retrieval quality, faithfulness and citation support together on prosthetics content. Those absences are the places where original data collection, a domain-specific RAG evaluation set and local validation would add evidence that the literature does not contain, provided the reuse licences and the input-dependent regulatory classification are settled first. The next verification step is mechanical: run Crossref, PubMed and Retraction Watch lookups for every DOI from an unrestricted machine, and open the unread primary papers and the official VA upper-limb PDF, before the registry is frozen.
